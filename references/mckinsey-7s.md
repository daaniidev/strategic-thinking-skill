# McKinsey 7S: internal organizational design

The 7S model doesn't analyze the market or the competition: it analyzes
whether an organization's **internal** elements — how it's organized, how it
operates, and how it thinks — are aligned with each other. It was developed
at McKinsey in the 1980s to explain why a strategy that's well designed on
paper sometimes fails to work: not because the strategy was bad, but because
the rest of the organization wasn't built to support it.

## The 7 S's

They're split into "hard" elements — easy to identify and change by decree —
and "soft" elements — harder to change because they're cultural and depend
on people.

| Type | S | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| Hard | **Strategy** | The plan for achieving competitive advantage and reaching the objectives |
| Hard | **Structure** | How the company is organized: hierarchy, departments, reporting lines |
| Hard | **Systems** | Day-to-day processes and procedures: IT, finance, HR, workflows |
| Soft | **Skills** | The competencies and capabilities the organization actually masters |
| Soft | **Staff** | Who makes up the team, and how talent is hired, developed, and retained |
| Soft | **Style** | How leadership actually leads and behaves in practice, beyond what it says it does |
| Soft | **Shared values** | The culture and principles that bind the organization together; the center of the model, on which the other six depend |

In McKinsey's original diagram, Shared values sits at the center: the other
six S's are arranged around it, because values are what give the rest their
coherence.

## How to use it

The goal is **not** to fill in the 7 boxes as a descriptive checklist. The
model's value lies in spotting **misalignments**: points where one S pulls
in one direction and another pulls in the opposite direction. The classic
example is a company that defines a new international expansion strategy
but keeps a structure, systems, and skills built for operating as a small,
local business — the strategy is well written, but the rest of the
organization can't execute it.

**Example:** a B2B software company announces a strategic shift toward
consultative selling for large accounts (**Strategy**), but its commission
system still rewards fast, low-ticket deal volume (**Systems**), and the
most influential salespeople on the team are the ones who are best at that
old transactional sales model (**Skills**, **Staff**). The sales team,
rationally, keeps optimizing for what pays their commission — the new
strategy stays on paper while Systems and Staff remain aligned with the old
one.

Guiding questions to audit alignment, S by S:

- **Does this S support the current strategy, or is it still designed for a
  previous one?** (Structure, Systems)
- **Do the staff and skills available today allow the strategy's
  requirements to be executed, or is there a capability gap?** (Skills, Staff)
- **Do the leadership style and shared values reinforce the change being
  asked for, or do they contradict it in day-to-day practice?** (Style,
  Shared values)

When an answer points to a gap, that's the S to work on — not all seven at
once.

## When to use it

Apply 7S when the request is about **internal organizational design**:
reviewing whether an organization's structure, systems, or culture are
aligned, or diagnosing why a strategy isn't fully executing internally
despite being well framed.

Don't use it for external market decisions — competition, industry
attractiveness, business model validation. For those, see
`references/porter-canvas.md` (Porter, Business Model Canvas) or
`references/swot-pestel.md` (SWOT, PESTEL).

## Closing note

7S is normally applied **after** the strategy or objective it needs to
support is already clear (see `references/strategy-fundamentals.md` or
whatever external framework was used to set direction — Porter, BMC,
SWOT/PESTEL). 7S exists to check that the organization can sustain a
strategy, not to replace it.

Avoid the anti-pattern of running a 7S analysis without first defining
which strategy it needs to support: without that reference point, there's
nothing to measure Structure, Systems, or Skills against, and the exercise
ends up as a description of the company with no actionable conclusions.
