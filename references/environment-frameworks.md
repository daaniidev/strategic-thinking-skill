# Environment frameworks: VUCA and BANI

Both frameworks are **diagnostic** tools: they describe the nature of the
environment an organization faces before deciding what to do about it. They
are not interchangeable — VUCA reads the market and the external
environment, BANI reads people and internal dynamics.

## VUCA

Volatile, Uncertain, Complex, Ambiguous. It originated in the military (US
Army War College) and was adopted in corporate strategy to describe
fast-changing, unpredictable environments.

| Letter | Meaning | Practical implication |
|---|---|---|
| **V**olatile | Change is fast and its magnitude is unpredictable | Long-term plans become obsolete quickly; strategy needs to be reviewed more often |
| **U**ncertain | Reliable information about what will happen is lacking | Forecasts lose their power; it's better to work with scenarios than a single projection |
| **C**omplex | Many interconnected variables, cause-and-effect is hard to isolate | Simple solutions fail; systemic analysis is needed, not just linear thinking |
| **A**mbiguous | There's a lack of clarity about what events mean or how to interpret them | The same data supports multiple readings; hypotheses need to be tested rather than assumed as certainties |

**When to use it:** when analyzing the external business environment —
market, competition, technology, regulation — when the concern is the
unpredictability and speed of change outside the organization.

**Example:** a retail company that sees new online sales channels and
regulatory changes emerge in the same quarter, rendering its annual
expansion plan outdated within two months — a classic VUCA situation: market
volatility plus regulatory uncertainty.

## BANI

Brittle, Anxious, Nonlinear, Incomprehensible. Proposed by Jamais Cascio as
an evolution of VUCA to capture dynamics that VUCA doesn't explain well:
sudden collapses of seemingly stable systems and the psychological toll of
living through constant change. Its focus is more internal and human than
market-driven.

| Letter | Meaning | Practical implication |
|---|---|---|
| **B**rittle | Systems that look strong but can break suddenly without warning | Optimizing for efficiency isn't enough; margin and redundancy need to be built in |
| **A**nxious | Uncertainty generates anxiety that paralyzes decision-making | People avoid deciding or overreact; emotional management is needed, not just information |
| **N**onlinear | Small causes produce disproportionate effects, and vice versa | Linear planning models fail; results need to be tolerated even when they aren't proportional to the effort |
| **I**ncomprehensible | Too much information, arriving too fast to process meaningfully | Seeking total certainty backfires; ambiguity needs to be accepted and communication simplified |

**When to use it:** when diagnosing internal dynamics — organizational
climate, resistance to change, burnout, culture — rather than the external
market. It's the right framework when the problem isn't "we don't know
what's going to happen in the market" but "the team is exhausted and
disengaged in the face of constant change."

**Example:** a workforce that, after several consecutive restructurings,
shows high turnover and low morale even though business metrics look
healthy — the organizational system is brittle despite appearing solid, and
anxiety about the next change blocks productivity. A BANI situation, not
VUCA.

## When to choose one over the other

| Criterion | Use VUCA when... | Use BANI when... |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | The problem is the market, competition, or the macro environment | The problem is people, culture, or the internal organization |
| Dominant symptom | Unpredictability and lack of information to decide | Fragility of systems and emotional strain on the team |
| Type of response sought | Scenarios, competitive intelligence, strategic planning | Organizational resilience, change management, team wellbeing |

Don't mix them arbitrarily: if the user asks about the market, don't force
BANI; if they ask about the state of the team, don't force VUCA. In
situations that combine both dimensions (external and internal), both can
be applied, explicitly stating which part of the diagnosis each one covers.

## Note: diagnosis, not action

VUCA and BANI describe the environment but don't say what to do about it.
Once the environment has been characterized with one of these frameworks,
the usual next step is to move to an analysis that actually produces
actionable recommendations — SWOT or PESTEL. See
`references/swot-pestel.md`.

## Scenario planning

Uncertainty (the "U" in VUCA) has a specific planning technique for working
through it, rather than settling for the generic idea that "it's better to
work with scenarios than a single projection": scenario planning (the
classic Shell/GBN method). It's the answer to the question "VUCA/BANI
diagnose the environment as uncertain, but what do I do about it?"

**What it is**: instead of projecting a single future, 2-4 plausible,
divergent narratives are built about how the environment could evolve, in
order to pre-test the strategy against each one before committing to a
single plan.

**When to use it**: very high uncertainty, 3-10 year time horizons, large
and irreversible decisions (market entry, capital investment,
merger/acquisition, business model transformation), or when different
stakeholders disagree about "what's going to happen."

**Difference from the decision tree** (see `decision-making.md`): a decision
tree maps a company's own options and their probabilities/expected values
at known branch points and calculates an expected value; scenario planning
maps plausible external futures (outside the company's control) and doesn't
calculate an expected value — instead it builds qualitative narratives to
test the robustness of the strategy.

### Steps

1. Identify the focal question / strategic decision driving the exercise.
2. List driving forces (social, technological, economic, regulatory
   trends).
3. Separate predetermined elements (what's practically certain) from
   critical uncertainties (what's genuinely uncertain and high-impact).
4. Select the 2 most relevant and mutually independent critical
   uncertainties (more than 2-3 makes the number of scenarios grow
   exponentially and lose usefulness).
5. Build a 2x2 matrix crossing those two uncertainties → 4 scenarios (or
   reduce to 2-3 if similar quadrants are grouped together).
6. Write up each scenario as a plausible narrative: a memorable name,
   internally coherent logic, and early signals that would indicate that
   future is materializing.
7. Pre-test the current strategy (or the options under evaluation) against
   each scenario: does it survive? Which decisions are robust across all
   scenarios ("no-regret moves") and which are bets specific to a single
   scenario?
8. Monitor signals to track which scenario reality is moving toward and
   adjust accordingly.

**Guiding questions**:
- Which two uncertainties, if resolved today, would change our strategy the
  most — and are they truly independent of each other?
- Of the actions we're considering, which ones work well across all four
  scenarios (no-regret moves) and which only make sense if a specific
  scenario plays out?
- What observable early signals would tell us, within 6-12 months, which
  scenario we're moving toward?
