# SMART Goals and Deployment with OKR

## 1. SMART: the five conditions of a well-formed goal

Before applying any strategic analysis framework, the goal being pursued must
be well written. SMART is the quality filter: a goal that doesn't pass all
five letters isn't ready to plan around.

| Letter | Requires the goal to... |
|---|---|
| **S**pecific | State unambiguously what is to be achieved — avoid vague verbs ("improve", "grow") without a concrete object. |
| **M**easurable | Have a metric or indicator that lets you know, without debate, whether it has been met. |
| **A**chievable | Be realistic given the available resources, time, and context — ambitious, not fanciful. |
| **R**elevant | Be connected to a real business or team priority, not a goal set by inertia. |
| **T**ime-bound | Have a deadline or defined timeframe — without a date, there is no urgency and no way to evaluate progress in time. |

### Before / after

**Poorly written:** "We want to improve online sales."

It's vague (improve by how much, for which product?), not objectively
measurable, doesn't say whether it's achievable, doesn't make clear why it's
a priority now, and has no deadline.

**Well written:** "Increase online store revenue in the Spanish market by 15%
over the next quarter, without increasing the marketing budget."

- Specific: online store revenue, Spanish market.
- Measurable: 15%.
- Achievable: the available resource is capped (same budget), which forces
  validation that the target is realistic before it's set.
- Relevant: assumed to be connected to a higher-level business objective
  (growth in the digital channel).
- Time-bound: next quarter.

## 2. SMART is not enough for ongoing tracking

SMART solves a specific problem: it defines **one** goal well, at a given
point in time. But it says nothing about how that goal is tracked week to
week or month to month, how it's distributed across teams, or what to do if
the indicator drifts off course halfway through. A well-written SMART goal
can sit in a document unreviewed until the deadline — by which point it's
already too late to correct course.

When the user's question isn't "how do I define this objective?" but "how do
I track this over time?" or "how do I break it down by quarter or by team?",
SMART is no longer sufficient and it's worth deploying it via OKR.

## 3. OKR: deploying a SMART goal into key results (supplementary content)

OKR (*Objectives and Key Results*) doesn't replace SMART — it uses it as an
input. The **Objective** is the goal itself, formulated in the same SMART
spirit (specific, relevant, time-bound). The **Key Results** (2 to 4 per
objective) are the measurable indicators that, if all are met, guarantee the
objective has been achieved. A Key Result is always an outcome, never a task:
"launch the campaign" is not a valid Key Result; "get 10,000 visits from the
campaign" is.

**Example** (building on the goal from point 1):

- **Objective:** Increase online store revenue in Spain this quarter.
- **Key Result 1:** Online store revenue up 15% versus the previous quarter.
- **Key Result 2:** Checkout conversion rate from 2.1% to 2.8%.
- **Key Result 3:** Average order value from €45 to €50.
- **Key Result 4:** Cart abandonment rate below 60%.

Each Key Result is reviewed periodically (weekly or monthly) throughout the
quarter, which allows deviations to be caught and corrected before the period
ends — something SMART, on its own, doesn't offer.

### Committed vs. aspirational: a distinction SMART doesn't have

SMART doesn't distinguish between types of goals — every SMART goal is
expected to be met at 100%, because the "Achievable" letter itself already
filtered out what isn't realistic. OKR does distinguish, and it's one of its
most practical ideas:

- **Committed OKR:** expected to be met at 100%. It has guaranteed priority
  for team resources and time; if it competes for resources with another
  objective mid-cycle, the committed one wins.
- **Aspirational OKR (moonshot):** deliberately designed to stretch beyond
  the comfortable. Achieving **0.7 (70%)** is already considered a healthy
  result — consistently hitting 100% is a sign the objective was set too low,
  not a sign of success. Precisely for that reason, it must be protected from
  competing for the same resources as committed objectives: if it competes
  and always loses, it stops being a real bet and becomes filler.

Confusing the two is a common mistake: treating an aspirational OKR as if it
had to reach 100% penalizes the team for taking a risk, and treating a
committed OKR as aspirational (accepting a 0.7) lowers the bar on what
actually needs to be delivered.

### Review cadence

OKR isn't reviewed only at the close of the period:

- **Periodic check-ins** (weekly or biweekly) during the cycle, to update
  progress on each Key Result and decide whether a course correction is
  needed while there's still time to react.
- **Closing score** at the end of the quarter, on a **0 to 1.0** scale per
  Key Result (and per Objective, as an average of its Key Results) — not a
  simple met/not-met.
- Many organizations (Google is the most cited example) run an **annual
  strategic cycle** in parallel — longer-horizon objectives, reviewed less
  frequently — alongside the **quarterly operating cycle**, which is
  reviewed through check-ins and closes with scoring.

### Why OKR is more than a writing format

SMART is a writing format for a single goal, and its priority is that the
goal be realistic ("Achievable"). OKR is a full management system: it sets
goals, aligns them across teams, makes them transparent, and defines a review
cadence — and it deliberately seeks stretch goals, where falling short of
100% is acceptable (even expected) for aspirational ones. OKR is also
designed to cascade with **full transparency**: any person's or team's OKRs
are visible to the rest of the organization, not just to whoever set them. A
SMART goal, by contrast, typically lives within a single team or an isolated
document, without that cross-visibility by design.

**When to use it:** when the user explicitly asks how to track something
continuously, how to break a goal down by quarter or by team, or how to know
if it's "on track" before the deadline arrives. If they're only asking for
help defining or validating a single goal, SMART is enough — don't force OKR
on top of it.

### Guiding questions when deploying to OKR

- Should this objective be treated as **committed** (100% is required and it
  has priority for resources) or as **aspirational** (0.7 is already success,
  and it must not compete for the same resources as committed objectives)?
- Is each Key Result a measurable outcome, or is it actually a task disguised
  as an outcome ("launch", "implement", "design")?
- What cadence will check-ins follow during the quarter — weekly or
  biweekly — and who reviews them?

## 4. Closing note: sequence, not alternative

SMART and OKR don't compete with each other, nor are they two different ways
of solving the same problem: they are sequential steps. First, SMART is used
to validate that the goal is well formulated — specific, measurable,
achievable, relevant, and time-bound. Only afterward, if it needs to be
deployed over time or distributed across teams, is that goal turned into an
Objective with its Key Results through OKR. Never the reverse: defining Key
Results on top of a poorly formulated objective only moves the ambiguity to
a different level.
