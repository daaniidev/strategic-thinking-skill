# Innovation and growth: Blue Ocean, Three Horizons, JTBD, and Innovator's Dilemma

## Blue Ocean Strategy (Kim & Mauborgne)

Blue Ocean doesn't compare the organization with the competition within an
industry's existing rules: it questions the rules themselves. Its thesis is
that competing within an already-defined industry ("red ocean") leads to a
zero-sum war over the same value factors, while it's possible to create a
new market space ("blue ocean") with no direct competition, breaking the
usual trade-off between low cost and high differentiation.

### The strategy canvas and the value curve

The strategy canvas is a chart with two axes:

- **X-axis** — the factors the industry currently competes on: price,
  quality, variety, service, speed, brand, etc. (usually between 5 and 8
  factors).
- **Y-axis** — the level of offering (low → high) each competitor gives to
  each factor.

Connecting a competitor's points across the factors produces its **value
curve**. When the value curves of all competitors in an industry look
similar, it's the visual sign of a red ocean: no one is really
differentiated, they're just competing on intensity over the same factors.

### The four actions framework (ERRC grid)

To draw a divergent value curve, Blue Ocean proposes questioning every
factor on the canvas with four questions, organized in an
Eliminate-Reduce-Raise-Create (ERRC) matrix:

| Action | Question | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Eliminate** | Which factors that the industry takes for granted should be eliminated? | Lowers cost |
| **Reduce** | Which factors should be reduced well below the industry standard? | Lowers cost |
| **Raise** | Which factors should be raised well above the industry standard? | Raises perceived value |
| **Create** | Which factors should be created that the industry has never offered? | Raises perceived value |

Eliminate and Reduce lower the cost structure; Raise and Create increase
the value perceived by the customer. By acting on all four actions at once,
instead of just raising or lowering cost, the aim is to pursue low cost and
high differentiation simultaneously — the trade-off Porter considers almost
always irreconcilable.

**What it's for**: finding an uncontested market space instead of fighting
for share in a saturated one, by redesigning the value proposition instead
of optimizing it marginally.

**When to use it**: it kicks in once a SWOT or Porter's Five Forces have
already confirmed the industry is saturated, with high rivalry and margins
under pressure, and "competing better at the same thing" has limited room
left. It doesn't replace Porter as a diagnosis — it uses that diagnosis as
the starting point for deciding it's time to leave the industry as
currently defined.

### Guiding questions

- What are the 5-8 factors your industry currently competes on, and what
  level (low/medium/high) does each relevant competitor give to each one?
- Which factors would you eliminate or reduce without the "non-customer"
  (someone who currently buys from no one in the industry) missing them?
  What new factor would solve a real pain point of that non-customer that
  no one in the industry addresses?
- Your resulting value curve, drawn on the canvas: is it clearly divergent
  from your competitors', or does it end up looking like the industry
  average after all?

## McKinsey's Three Horizons of growth

The Three Horizons model manages growth as a portfolio across three time
bands of distinct risk and maturity, instead of judging every growth
initiative by the same yardstick.

| Horizon | Timeframe | Nature | Goal |
|---|---|---|---|
| **H1** | 0-2 years | Core business, already at scale | Defend and extend; funds H2 and H3 |
| **H2** | 2-5 years | Emerging businesses with visible product-market fit | Scale |
| **H3** | 5-10 years | Future options: early ideas, experiments | Validate with low resource commitment |

**Key principle**: the three horizons are managed **simultaneously, not
sequentially** — H1 isn't exhausted first before "moving on" to H2. Each
horizon needs its own metrics and risk expectations: an H3 bet is judged on
learning and validation, not on the ROI or margin demanded of H1. Applying
a mature core business's KPIs to an H3 bet kills it before it has a chance
to prove anything.

**What it's for**: it provides the portfolio and time-horizon lens that
decides *when* and *with what level of rigor* to evaluate each growth
initiative, beyond just what type of growth it is.

**How it complements the other frameworks in this skill**: Ansoff
(`porter-canvas.md`) gives the *options* for growth (penetration, product
development, market development, diversification) but doesn't say when or
with what risk horizon to pursue each one; McKinsey 7S (`mckinsey-7s.md`)
diagnoses the organization as it is today. The Three Horizons add the
missing dimension: how much investment and attention goes to each
timeframe, and with what expectations.

### Guiding questions

- What percentage of your investment and attention currently goes to H1,
  H2, and H3 respectively, and does that split reflect where you want to
  be in 5 years, or does it only defend the present?
- Which H2 initiative already has real evidence of product-market fit and
  now needs more resources to scale, not more experimentation?
- Which H3 bet are you killing (or letting die) by measuring it against the
  KPIs demanded of the core business?

## Jobs to be Done (JTBD), strategic use

JTBD starts from the premise that the customer doesn't "buy a product":
they hire a solution to make specific progress in a given circumstance —
the "job." A job has three dimensions: **functional** (the task to solve),
**emotional** (how the customer wants to feel, or avoid feeling), and
**social** (how they want to be perceived).

The job, not the product category, is the stable unit for defining the real
competitive space: the true competitors are **any solution that gets
"hired" for the same job**, including non-consumption (doing nothing,
solving it with a manual method, delegating it informally...). This is also
useful for deciding where to innovate: which job *outcomes* are
**underserved** (the customer can't find anything that solves them well)
and which are **overserved** (expensive or complex solutions over-solve
something that was already solved).

**Job Story format**, useful for articulating the job operationally:

> When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome].

### Strategic use vs. use in customer research

It's important to distinguish two uses of JTBD that are frequently
confused:

- **Use in customer research** (not the focus of this section): segmenting
  customers by the job they solve, mapping the "hiring" and "firing"
  journey of a solution. It's descriptive and looks at the current
  customer. See `references/decision-making.md` or the `customer-research`
  skill for that use.
- **Strategic use** (the one relevant here): using the job to **decide
  market and growth opportunity**, with more precision than Ansoff — which
  crosses product × market but never explicitly asks "what job is this
  hired for?" Redefining the market by the job solved, instead of by the
  product category, changes what counts as competition and where the real
  innovation opportunities are.

**What it's for**: broadening the diagnosis of "who is my competition" and
"where is there room to grow" beyond the limits imposed by the product
category itself.

**When to use it**: when Ansoff has already identified a growth direction
but the underlying question is still open — "are we correctly defining
what counts as a 'new market' or 'new product' for our customers?" — or
when there's a need to understand what the organization truly competes
against beyond its obvious category competitors.

### Guiding questions

- If your customer "fired" your product tomorrow, what other solution —
  including doing nothing — would they hire to make the same progress?
- Which part of the job (functional, emotional, or social) is overserved
  by expensive or complex solutions, and which is underserved for lack of
  a simple alternative?
- If you redefined your market by "the job you solve" instead of by your
  product category, does your universe of opportunities and competitors
  change?

## Innovator's Dilemma / disruption (Clayton Christensen)

Distinguishes two types of innovation: **sustaining**, which improves the
attributes the mainstream customer already values, in pursuit of higher
margin; and **disruptive**, which is initially inferior on those same
attributes but better at something new (price, simplicity, access), enters
through the low end or non-consumption, and over time improves enough to
invade the mainstream market. The dilemma is that a market leader's
resource-allocation processes — oriented toward better serving today's
profitable customer — systematically reject investing in the disruptive
option, because it looks like a worse business, until it's already too
late to respond.

### Guiding questions

- Is the competitor or technology you're evaluating "worse" on what your
  main customer measures, but better on price, simplicity, or access for a
  segment you currently ignore?
- If you created a separate unit to respond to that threat, would your own
  prioritization processes end up killing it anyway, for competing poorly
  against the core business's ROI?
- Which of today's "non-consumers" could be the initial market for a
  disruptive response of your own?

## How the four relate to each other, and to Ansoff / Porter's Five Forces

The four frameworks in this file approach innovation and growth from
different angles, and build on the ones already in this skill:

- **Porter's Five Forces** diagnose how attractive and contested an
  industry is as currently defined. When that diagnosis reveals a
  saturated industry with margins under pressure, **Blue Ocean** offers the
  way out: redefining the rules of competition instead of playing the same
  game better.
- **Ansoff** gives the four structural growth options (product × market).
  **JTBD** sharpens that decision by asking "what job is this hired for?",
  which often reveals that Ansoff's "new" market or product wasn't so new
  after all, or that there's an opportunity Ansoff couldn't see because it
  was anchored to the current product category. **Innovator's Dilemma**
  explains why an established organization tends to systematically ignore
  exactly the non-consumption opportunities that JTBD helps identify.
- **Three Horizons** doesn't compete with the other three: it organizes
  them over time. A Blue Ocean opportunity, a JTBD-driven redefinition, or
  a disruptive bet almost always start out as H3 or H2, not H1, and must be
  evaluated with the metrics and risk level of their own horizon, not those
  of the core business.

In summary: Porter and SWOT diagnose the starting point; Ansoff enumerates
the possible directions for growth; JTBD and Blue Ocean question whether
those directions are well defined or whether there's an uncontested space
neither one sees; Innovator's Dilemma explains the organizational
resistance to pursuing them; and Three Horizons provides the portfolio
discipline to manage them all at once without holding them to the same
performance bar.
