# Competitive analysis and business model: Porter, Business Model Canvas, and Ansoff

## Porter's Five Forces

Porter's Five Forces describe an industry's competitive structure: they
don't analyze a specific competitor, but rather how attractive and intense
the entire "playing field" is, and where the pressure on margins comes
from.

1. **Competitive rivalry** — the intensity of competition among the players
   already present in the industry. *Guiding question:* how many
   competitors are there, how similar is their offering, and to what
   extent do they compete on price?
2. **Threat of new entrants** — how easily new players can enter the
   industry and take market share. *Guiding question:* what barriers to
   entry exist (capital, regulation, network effects, brand)?
3. **Supplier power** — suppliers' ability to dictate price or terms.
   *Guiding question:* how many alternative suppliers are there, and how
   costly is it to switch suppliers?
4. **Buyer power** — customers' ability to push prices down or demand more
   value. *Guiding question:* how concentrated are buyers, and how easily
   can they switch suppliers?
5. **Threat of substitution** — the risk that a different solution, not
   necessarily a direct competitor, meets the same need. *Guiding
   question:* what alternative outside the industry could solve the same
   customer problem?

**What it's for:** identifying where to create value, where to reduce or
differentiate costs relative to the competition, and as the basis for a
comprehensive structural review of the industry (not just a tactical
adjustment).

**When to use it:** when the user's question centers on a market or
industry's competitive structure — "is this a good industry to enter?",
"why are our margins under pressure?", "who holds the bargaining power
here?".

## Business Model Canvas

The Canvas doesn't analyze competition: it designs or validates an
organization's entire business model, block by block. The 9 blocks are
grouped into three zones around the value proposition, which sits at the
center.

**Center — what we offer**

| Block | What it answers |
|---|---|
| Value proposition | What problem do we solve or what need do we meet, and why do customers choose this over something else? |

**Customer side (right) — who we reach and how**

| Block | What it answers |
|---|---|
| Customer segments | Who do we create value for? What are the most important customer groups? |
| Customer relationships | What kind of relationship does each segment expect, and how is it maintained (self-service, personal, community)? |
| Channels | How does the value proposition reach the customer (sales, distribution, communication)? |

**Operations side (left) — what we need to produce it**

| Block | What it answers |
|---|---|
| Key activities | What essential activities does the business model require? |
| Key resources | What assets (physical, human, intellectual, financial) are indispensable? |
| Key partners | What suppliers and partners make the model possible, and what gets outsourced? |

**Foundation — the economics of the model**

| Block | What it answers |
|---|---|
| Revenue streams | What is each segment willing to pay for, and how do they pay? |
| Cost structure | What are the model's most important costs, and what drives them? |

**What it's for:** designing a new business model or validating/challenging
an existing one holistically, not just its position relative to the
competition.

**When to use it:** when the question isn't "how do we compare to the
competition?" but "does this business make sense the way it's set up?" —
launching a new model, pivoting, or a full review of how value is created
and captured.

## Ansoff Matrix (extra)

The Ansoff Matrix doesn't analyze competition or the business model itself:
it maps an organization's growth options by crossing product
(current/new) with market (current/new).

| | Current product | New product |
|---|---|---|
| **Current market** | Market penetration — sell more of the same to existing customers | Product development — new offerings for the current customer base |
| **New market** | Market development — the current product, but in a new market, segment, or geography | Diversification — new product in a new market, the highest risk and the biggest change of direction |

**When to use it:** specifically when the user is talking about
**expansion into new markets** or **growth** — it's the canonical example
of a long-term strategic decision within this skill, because it forces you
to explicitly name which axis is moving (product, market, or both) and to
size the risk before committing.

## Closing note

Porter looks outward: the industry's competitive pressure. The Canvas looks
at the whole business: how value is created, delivered, and captured.
Ansoff looks forward: where to grow. They aren't mutually exclusive — a
single analysis can touch all three — but rarely are all three needed at
once. Choose based on the user's specific question: industry competition →
Porter; does the model make sense? → Canvas; where do we grow? → Ansoff.
