# Stakeholders and Risk

This file covers three convergence-and-control frameworks: mapping who is
affected by or can block a decision (Mendelow), prioritizing which
initiatives to execute first (impact/effort), and quantifying the risk of the
options chosen (risk matrix). They don't replace SWOT/PESTEL or the option
generation covered in `references/decision-making.md` — they build on them.

## Mendelow Matrix (power/interest)

A 2x2 matrix that classifies an initiative's stakeholders along two axes:
their **power** to influence the outcome (Y axis, low→high) and their
**interest** in whether the initiative moves forward or not (X axis,
low→high).

| | Low interest | High interest |
|---|---|---|
| **High power** | Keep satisfied | Manage closely |
| **Low power** | Monitor (minimum effort) | Keep informed |

- **Manage closely** (high power, high interest): these are the people who
  can make or break the initiative and are also following it closely. They
  need direct, frequent engagement.
- **Keep satisfied** (high power, low interest): they can block if something
  bothers them, but don't follow the day-to-day. Inform them just enough
  that they don't turn against the initiative.
- **Keep informed** (low power, high interest): they don't decide, but their
  opinion carries weight with those who do, or they can become vocal
  advocates or opponents. Keep them up to date without spending the same
  effort on them as on "manage closely".
- **Monitor** (low power, low interest): minimum effort — passive watch in
  case their position changes.

**What it's for**: when a strategic initiative (merger, restructuring,
launch, organizational change) involves several actors with different
interests, this matrix keeps you from treating them all the same and
concentrates management effort where it can actually move the outcome.

**When to use it**: it's an input that precedes or runs parallel to
SWOT/PESTEL, not a substitute for it — while SWOT diagnoses the situation,
Mendelow diagnoses the people and organizations around the decision. It's
also the natural foundation for the change management already described in
`references/decision-making.md` (Kotter's 8-Step Change Process): before
"communicating" or "anticipating resistance," you need to know who to manage
closely and who to just keep informed.

### Guiding questions

- Who can approve, fund, or block this decision, and who merely experiences
  or comments on it without real power over it?
- For each "manage closely" stakeholder, what do they need to see or hear to
  avoid becoming a blocker?
- Are there currently low-power stakeholders who might gain power if the
  initiative moves forward (for example, a team that doesn't decide today
  but will end up managing the resulting budget)?

## Impact/effort matrix

A 2x2 matrix that classifies already-generated initiatives or options by
their expected **impact** (Y axis, low→high) and the **effort** they require
(X axis, low→high).

| | Low effort | High effort |
|---|---|---|
| **High impact** | Quick Win (do now) | Major Project / big bet (plan) |
| **Low impact** | Fill-In (if capacity allows) | Avoid / resource trap |

- **Quick Win**: high impact with little effort — execute now, without
  waiting for a planning cycle.
- **Major Project / big bet**: high impact but high effort — worth the
  investment, but needs planning, sponsorship, and a timeline.
- **Fill-In**: low impact and low effort — only if there's spare capacity,
  never at the expense of a Quick Win or a Major Project.
- **Avoid / resource trap**: low impact and high effort — consumes resources
  without returning proportional value; this is the cell that requires the
  most discipline to rule out.

**What it's for**: it's the natural convergence step after generating options
(brainstorming, cross-functional teams, design thinking — see "Generating
ideas before deciding" in `references/decision-making.md`). Generate the
options unfiltered first, then use this matrix afterward to decide the order
of execution — never the other way around.

### Guiding questions

- If we could only execute 3 initiatives this quarter, which ones would they
  be and why?
- Does the "effort" we're estimating include only hours and cost, or also
  organizational complexity and cross-team dependencies?
- Is any initiative classified as a "Major Project" actually a technical or
  organizational prerequisite for others to work (and therefore not
  optional even though its direct impact looks smaller)?

## Risk matrix (probability × impact), at the strategic level

A matrix that cross-references the **probability** of a risk materializing
(X axis, low/medium/high or a 1-5 scale) with its **impact** if it occurs (Y
axis, low/medium/high or a 1-5 scale). The product of both axes gives a
**score** (Probability × Impact) that places the risk in a green, yellow, or
red zone.

Unlike a project risk matrix (timeline, budget, scope of a specific
deliverable), the risk here is **strategic**: risk from a decision, from a
market choice, from dependency on a key supplier or partner, reputational
risk, or regulatory risk — things that compromise the organization's
position, not just a single project.

**Responses by risk zone:**

| Response | When it applies |
|---|---|
| **Avoid** | Eliminate the cause of the risk directly — usually by dropping the strategic option that generates it. Reserved for red-zone risks with a clear, avoidable cause. |
| **Mitigate** | Reduce the probability or the impact (not necessarily both) through direct action. The most common response for yellow- or red-zone risks that can't be avoided without losing the associated opportunity. |
| **Transfer** | Pass the risk to a third party — insurance, contractual clause, joint venture, risk-sharing with a partner. Useful when the risk is real but another party can absorb it better or more cheaply. |
| **Contingency** | Define a response plan in advance in case the risk materializes, without acting on the cause yet. Suitable for low-probability, high-impact risks, where acting now would be premature but having no plan would be negligent. |
| **Accept** | Consciously take on the risk, with monitoring but no active action. Reserved for low-score risks (green zone). |

**What it's for**: it's the natural next step after SWOT — SWOT's Threats
are qualitative ("what could go wrong"); the risk matrix quantifies them by
probability and impact so you can prioritize which ones demand immediate
action and which only need watching.

### Guiding questions

- If this risk materializes tomorrow, what impact does it have on revenue,
  reputation, or business continuity, and is it reversible?
- Is the probability we're assigning based on data (historical data,
  industry benchmarks), or an untested intuition?
- For red-zone risks: do we mitigate, transfer, or simply avoid the
  strategic option that generates the risk? Who owns this risk (who is
  accountable if it materializes)?

## Recommended flow

The three frameworks in this file fit together in sequence within a single
strategic decision-making process:

1. **Map stakeholders** (Mendelow) — before deciding what to do, understand
   who can approve it, block it, or be affected by it.
2. **Generate and prioritize initiatives** (impact/effort) — once the options
   have been generated (see `references/decision-making.md`), rank them by
   what to execute first.
3. **Assess the risk of the top initiatives** (risk matrix) — for initiatives
   prioritized as "Quick Win" or "Major Project," quantify their strategic
   risk before committing resources.
