# Decision-making and change management

This file covers how to decide (process and models), how to generate options
before deciding, how that same process looks at a strategic and
organizational level, and how a decision that has already been made is
rolled out and sustained — including the change management that usually
comes with it.

## Decision-making process (7 steps)

Default starting point for any decision, tactical or strategic:

1. **Identify the decision** — pinpoint precisely what needs to be decided and why it needs deciding now.
2. **Gather information** — collect relevant data, context, and constraints before generating options.
3. **Identify alternatives** — list the possible paths forward (see "Generating ideas before deciding" if there aren't clear options yet).
4. **Evaluate alternatives** — weigh each option against the criteria and the expected risk/benefit.
5. **Make the decision** — explicitly choose an alternative, with clarity on who decides and with what authority.
6. **Implement** — execute the decision, assigning owners and timelines.
7. **Monitor and evaluate** — review results against expectations and adjust if needed.

## Decision-making models

Not every model fits every problem. The core criterion for choosing is the
**Cynefin framework**, which classifies the nature of the problem into four
domains (simple, complicated, complex, chaotic) and recommends a model type
from there:

| Model | What it is | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| **Rational** | Linear, analytical process: define the objective, evaluate all options against explicit criteria, choose the optimal one. | If the problem is **simple or complicated** (cause-effect is known or discoverable through analysis), use the rational model. |
| **Bounded rationality** | Recognizes there isn't enough time, information, or cognitive capacity to evaluate every option; the goal is a "good enough" solution, not the optimal one. | When the problem is complicated but the resources (time, data) for a full rational analysis are limited. |
| **Intuitive** | A decision based on experience and quickly recognized patterns, without an explicit analytical process. | If the problem is **complicated** and the decision-maker has accumulated experience in similar situations, or reaction time is short. |
| **Incremental** | The decision advances in small, reversible steps (pilot → validation → scale-up), adjusting along the way instead of committing all at once[^1]. | When uncertainty is high and the cost of a major mistake would be too steep; useful in complex domains where it pays to "test and learn." |
| **Vroom-Yetton** | A normative model that helps decide *how much* the team should participate in a decision (from autocratic to fully consultative/group-based), depending on the situation. | If the problem is **complicated and requires deciding the degree of team involvement** — when "how to decide" matters as much as "what to decide." |
| **Cynefin** | A framework for classifying problems as simple, complicated, complex, or chaotic, each calling for a different management response (best practices, expert analysis, experimentation, stabilizing action). | Use it first, as a filter, to determine which other model in this table applies. |
| **Six Thinking Hats** | De Bono's technique: the group analyzes the decision by rotating through six explicit hats/perspectives — white (objective data and facts), red (emotions and intuition), black (critical judgment, risks), yellow (optimism, benefits), green (creativity, alternatives), blue (process, meeting control). | If the problem is **complex** and several explicit perspectives are needed to avoid getting stuck on a single angle (e.g., purely analytical or purely optimistic). |
| **Decision trees** | A graphical representation of options, possible outcomes, and their probabilities/expected values. | For **decisions with quantifiable data** involving several chained rounds of decision-making (if X happens, then Y). |
| **Cost/benefit analysis** | A quantitative comparison of the expected costs and benefits of each alternative. | For **single-round decisions with quantifiable data**, especially when the choice needs to be justified in economic terms. |

[^1]: Real options reasoning (qualitative, without financial formulas): define exit/abandonment criteria ("kill criteria") for each stage in advance, and think about which "option" is being bought at each step — deferring, expanding, abandoning, or changing course. The rigorous version using financial valuation models falls outside the scope of this skill.

Practical rule: if the problem is simple or complicated and data is
available, use the rational model or cost/benefit analysis; if you also need
to decide how many people should be involved, add Vroom-Yetton; if the
problem is complex and ambiguous, use Six Thinking Hats to open up
perspectives before settling on a more analytical model; if it's chaotic,
act first to stabilize and decide afterward.

## Generating ideas before deciding

This step comes **before** "evaluate alternatives" in the 7-step process:
if there are no clear options on the table yet, there's no point moving on
to evaluating them. Five techniques, from most open-ended to most
structured:

- **Brainstorming** — free, fast generation of options as a group, without filtering yet. Use it when the solution space is completely open and quantity matters more than quality at this stage.
- **Comparative analysis** — placing several known options (your own or external benchmarks) side by side to see strengths and gaps. Use it when precedents or competitors already exist to learn from.
- **Delphi method** — successive, anonymous rounds of expert consultation, with aggregated feedback between rounds, until convergence. Use it when the problem requires expert judgment and you want to avoid groupthink or hierarchical influence.
- **Cross-functional teams** — bringing together people from different areas (product, operations, finance, etc.) so idea generation incorporates real constraints and perspectives from the start. Use it when the decision affects multiple areas and no single function has the full picture.
- **Design thinking** — a user-centered process (empathize, define, ideate, prototype, test) for generating solutions. Use it when the problem is poorly defined and it's worth understanding the experience of those affected before generating options.

## Extended strategic decision process

When the decision carries strategic weight (not a one-off choice), the
7-step process unfolds in more detail. These are two formulations of the
same process at different levels of granularity — the first is a
step-by-step extended version, the second is the high-level map for kicking
off a strategy planning effort from scratch.

**Key steps of the extended process:**

1. Environmental analysis — understand the external and internal context (this is where SWOT, PESTEL, or Porter fit in; see `references/swot-pestel.md` and `references/porter-canvas.md`).
2. Define objectives and criteria — what you want to achieve and how success will be measured (see `references/smart-goals.md`).
3. Brainstorming — generate options (see previous section).
4. Evaluate alternatives — weigh options against the defined criteria.
5. Scenario analysis — project how each alternative performs under different possible futures.
6. Stakeholder engagement — involve those who need to approve, execute, or are affected by the decision.
7. Decision-making — close out the choice explicitly.
8. Implementation planning — define how, who, and when it gets executed.

**Map for kicking off strategy planning (same process, starting-point view):**

1. Strategy process map — be clear upfront about which steps will be followed and in what order.
2. Problem identification — what is actually being solved.
3. Generate options and possibilities — open up the range of possible paths.
4. What would have to be true — make explicit the assumptions underlying each option so they can be validated.
5. Barriers to progress — identify what stands between the current situation and each option.
6. Choose the right option among several — close out the decision based on the above.

## Cascade process

Once the decision has been made, the cascade process describes how it is
rolled out and sustained over time within an organization — it's not a
method for deciding, but for making sure the decision survives
implementation:

1. **Alignment and clarity** — everyone understands the decision and why it was made.
2. **Empowerment and accountability** — clear ownership is assigned for each part of the execution.
3. **Integration and collaboration** — the teams involved coordinate with each other instead of working in silos.
4. **Communication and transparency** — progress and problems are shared openly, not hidden.
5. **Adaptability and flexibility** — execution is adjusted when reality diverges from the plan.
6. **Continuous learning improvement** — lessons are captured for the next decision.

## Strategic change management (Kotter's model)

When the decision involves organizational change — not just executing a
plan but changing how people work — the change itself needs to be managed.
This is **Kotter's change management model**, the famous "8 steps"
(*Leading Change*, HBR 1995 / book 1996), the standard term used for this
process in the management literature — it's worth naming it this way
because the other party is likely to recognize it under that name:

1. **Create a sense of urgency** — generate a compelling reason why the change can't wait.
2. **Build a guiding coalition** — assemble a group of leaders with enough power and influence to drive the change; this is the most distinctive step of Kotter's model compared with other change frameworks.
3. **Develop a vision and strategy** — articulate where the change is heading and how it will get there.
4. **Communicate the change vision** — repeatedly and through multiple channels, not just in the initial announcement.
5. **Empower broad-based action** — remove structural obstacles and give real autonomy to those who need to execute it.
6. **Generate short-term wins** — visible, early results that reinforce the credibility of the change.
7. **Consolidate gains and produce more change** — use the credibility gained to tackle the next systems or structures that haven't changed yet, without declaring victory too soon.
8. **Anchor new approaches in the culture** — institutionalize the change in norms, values, and leadership succession so it doesn't reverse.

In his later work (*Accelerate*, 2014), Kotter reframed these steps as
"accelerators" that can run in parallel rather than strictly sequentially,
through a "dual operating system."

**RACI for multi-team implementation.** When the "implementation planning"
phase (from the extended process) involves several teams, it helps to
support it with a **RACI** matrix — Responsible (executes), Accountable
(answers for the outcome), Consulted (provides input before the decision),
and Informed (finds out afterward) — for each task or deliverable of the
change. This avoids the ambiguity of "I thought the other team was handling
that," one of the most common reasons a well-designed change stalls during
execution.
