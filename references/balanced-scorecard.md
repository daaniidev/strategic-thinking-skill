# Balanced Scorecard (BSC)

The Balanced Scorecard (BSC), developed by Kaplan and Norton, is a
management and performance measurement system that translates an
organization's vision and strategy into objectives and metrics balanced
across **four perspectives**, instead of relying solely on financial
indicators. Financial indicators measure the past (what has already
happened) but say nothing about what is driving — or holding back — the
future; the BSC adds the perspectives that do.

## The 4 perspectives

| Perspective | Guiding question (Kaplan & Norton) | What it measures |
|---|---|---|
| **Financial** | How do we look to shareholders/investors? | Profitability, revenue growth, return |
| **Customer** | How do customers see us? | Satisfaction, retention, market share, perceived value proposition |
| **Internal processes** | Which processes must we excel at? | Operational efficiency, quality, innovation |
| **Learning and growth** | How do we sustain our ability to improve? | Talent, culture, systems, technology |

Each perspective breaks down into four elements: a strategic **objective**
(what we are pursuing), an **indicator**/KPI (how we measure it), a
**target** (what value counts as "achieved"), and an **initiative**/action
plan (what we do to achieve it).

## The strategy map: cause-and-effect relationships

The four perspectives are not four independent lists of KPIs: they are
linked by a causal relationship that flows bottom-up. This chain is what
Kaplan and Norton call the **strategy map**:

**Learning and growth** → enables → **Internal processes** → improves →
**Customer** → drives → **Financial**

In other words: investing in talent, culture, systems and technology
(Learning and growth) enables better execution of key processes (Internal
processes); more efficient, higher-quality processes improve the customer's
experience and perceived value proposition (Customer); and a more satisfied,
loyal customer base is what ultimately translates into revenue and
profitability (Financial). A financial objective that cannot be traced back
to a customer, process, or learning objective is disconnected from the rest
of the map — it's an isolated number, not part of a strategy.

## What it's for

Translating an already-defined strategy into an **operational measurement
system**: a set of objectives, indicators, targets and initiatives that a
team can review periodically to know whether the strategy is being executed
or not, well before the year's financial results confirm or deny it.

## When to use it

When the user **already has a defined strategy** — the result of a SWOT,
PESTEL, Porter's analysis, or Business Model Canvas exercise — and needs to
translate it into a system of tracking indicators. Triggered by questions
like "how do I measure whether the strategy is working?" or "how do I turn
the strategy into tracking indicators?"

Don't use it to *define* the strategy — that's what SWOT/PESTEL, Porter, or
the Business Model Canvas are for (`references/swot-pestel.md`,
`references/porter-canvas.md`) — nor to *validate* a single goal, see below.

## Difference from other frameworks in this skill

- **Vs. SMART** (`references/smart-goals.md`): SMART validates that **a
  single** goal is well formulated (specific, measurable, achievable,
  relevant, time-bound). The BSC is a complete system of indicators linked by
  cause and effect that covers the four perspectives of the whole
  organization, not a single goal in isolation. Each objective within a BSC
  perspective should, in turn, be well formulated using the SMART criteria.
- **Vs. OKR** (also in `references/smart-goals.md`): the BSC is organized
  around **business perspectives** with explicit causal relationships (the
  strategy map) and designed for a medium/long-term horizon. OKR (Objectives
  and Key Results) is organized around **ambitious objectives with key
  results**, designed for short cycles (typically quarterly) and agile
  cascading across teams. They are not mutually exclusive: they can coexist
  in the same management system — the BSC defines the underlying strategic
  perspectives and the indicators that matter over the medium/long term, and
  quarterly OKRs can feed and advance the indicators of each perspective
  cycle by cycle.
- **Vs. McKinsey 7S** (`references/mckinsey-7s.md`): 7S is an
  **organizational diagnostic** — whether an organization's structure,
  systems and culture are aligned with its strategy. The BSC is a
  **performance measurement system** — how to know whether the strategy is
  working. Different purposes: 7S diagnoses internal alignment, the BSC
  measures execution and results.
- **Vs. Business Model Canvas** (`references/porter-canvas.md`): the BMC
  describes and validates the business model (what is offered, to whom, how
  revenue is generated). The BSC takes that already-described business model
  and gives it a structured measurement system to track it over time.

## Guiding questions

For each of the four perspectives:

- What **strategic objective** are we pursuing in this perspective?
- What **indicator** (KPI) will we measure it with?
- What specific **target** makes us consider that objective "achieved"?
- What **initiative** or action plan will we put in place to achieve it?

And about the map as a whole:

- Is there a visible cause-and-effect relationship across the four
  perspectives in our map, or do we have financial objectives disconnected
  from the rest?
- Are we measuring only past results (the financial perspective, a lagging
  indicator) or also the future drivers (learning and growth, a leading
  indicator)? A scorecard made up only of lagging indicators warns too late.
