# Strategic narrative: communicating the plan

> **When to use this module**: only after a strategic analysis has already
> been completed (SWOT, PESTEL, 7S, a decision made, SMART goals defined...).
> Never before. You cannot narrate a strategy that does not yet exist — this
> module does not replace the analysis, it communicates it. That is why it is
> not applied by default: it is offered to the user at the close of the
> report (see section 4 of `SKILL.md`), and is only loaded and developed if
> the user says yes, they want to build the narrative.

The narrative is the tool that turns a sound analysis into something people
understand, remember and follow. A well-done SWOT can end up as a document
nobody rereads; a well-built narrative is what people retell afterward in
their own words. The narrative helps communicate complex ideas by connecting
emotionally and inspiring action — it does not replace analytical rigor, it
makes it transmissible.

## The 4 steps of the strategic narrative

**1. Define the hero**
The hero of the story is almost never the company — it is the team, the
customer, or the person living the problem the strategy solves. If the
"hero" is the company telling itself how well it is going to do, the
narrative sounds like self-promotion and fails to engage. Guiding question:
*whose story is this — who has something to gain or overcome, and it isn't
"us as a company"?*

**2. Outline the journey**
This is the current conflict or challenge: the tension that makes the
strategy necessary. This is where the narrative connects with the analysis
already done (the SWOT threat, the PESTEL factor applying pressure, the
structural weakness surfaced by 7S). Without a real, recognizable obstacle,
there is no journey to tell — only an announcement. Guiding question: *what
is costing, risking or blocking the hero right now, and why is the status
quo no longer an option?*

**3. Chart the destination**
This is what success looks like — and it has to be concrete and visualizable,
not a slogan. Not "we will be market leaders," but a scene: what the
customer does, what the team measures, what has changed in day-to-day life
once the strategy has worked. The sharper the image, the easier it is for
others to repeat it without distorting it. Guiding question: *if this works,
what is different a year from now that anyone can see or feel?*

**4. Bring the narrative to life**
This is the delivery: how it moves from script to real experience for the
audience — a presentation, a document, a meeting, a series of one-on-one
conversations. The choice of format is not a logistical detail, it is part
of the message: a decision that affects the whole workforce calls for a
meeting with room for questions, not just a PDF attached to an email.
Guiding question: *at what moment and in what format will this story live so
the audience experiences it, not just reads it?*

## Benefits

- **Clarity and understanding** — a story with a hero, a conflict and a
  destination is understood faster than a bulleted list of data; it reduces
  the cognitive effort of "translating" the strategy into something
  personally relevant.
- **Alignment and commitment** — when each person sees their own role in the
  hero's journey, it stops being "leadership's strategy" and becomes
  something they have an active part in.
- **Buy-in** — difficult decisions (cuts, shifting priorities,
  restructuring) meet less resistance when they are understood as part of a
  meaningful path, not as disconnected orders.

## 10 best practices for communicating strategy

1. **Start with the why** — explain the strategy's underlying reason before
   the what or the how; without the why, the rest sounds like arbitrary
   instructions.
2. **Keep it simple and clear** — if it doesn't fit in two or three
   jargon-free sentences, it probably isn't distilled enough yet to
   communicate.
3. **Tailor the message to the audience** — the same plan is told
   differently to a technical team, an investor, or a customer; change the
   emphasis, not the facts.
4. **Use multiple channels and formats** — a meeting, a document and an
   informal conversation reinforce the message in different ways; none on
   its own is enough for it to stick.
5. **Tell a compelling story** — apply the 4 steps above instead of
   presenting only conclusions and figures.
6. **Encourage dialogue and feedback** — leave explicit room for questions
   and objections; a strategy that doesn't allow questions produces surface
   compliance, not commitment.
7. **Lead by example** — the actions of whoever is leading must reflect the
   strategy they're communicating, or the narrative falls apart on its own.
8. **Provide regular updates** — communicating strategy is not a one-time
   launch event; the narrative needs further chapters or it gets forgotten.
9. **Be transparent and honest** — acknowledge the risks and what is still
   unknown; hiding the hard parts of the journey undermines credibility for
   the rest of it.
10. **Celebrate milestones and wins** — mark progress along the way to the
    destination; it makes it tangible that the story is moving forward, not
    stuck at the initial presentation.

## Closing

Who is listening to the narrative changes how these practices are applied:
an internal team needs more dialogue and regular updates; an investor
weighs clarity of the destination and honesty about risks more heavily; a
customer responds better to a hero they can directly identify with. Before
bringing the narrative to life, confirm with the user who the primary
audience is and adjust tone, level of detail and channel accordingly.
